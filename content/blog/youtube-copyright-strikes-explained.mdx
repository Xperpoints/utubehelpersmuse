## Quick answer

A YouTube copyright strike is a formal takedown notice from a rights holder: your video is removed and a strike goes on your channel's record. Three strikes within 90 days and YouTube terminates your channel. It's different from a Content ID claim, which is usually automated and doesn't penalize your channel. Avoid strikes by using only content you created, licensed, or have clear rights to — and never assume short clips or credit lines protect you.

## What's the difference between a copyright claim and a copyright strike?

This is the single most important distinction in YouTube copyright, and most creators learn it the confusing way — staring at a scary-looking email. They're completely different things.

**A Content ID claim** is automated. YouTube's Content ID system scans uploads against a database of reference files submitted by rights holders. When it finds a match — a song, a video clip, even a few seconds of background audio — it applies the rights holder's chosen policy. That policy is usually one of three things: monetize the video (ad revenue goes to them, not you), track the video's stats, or block it in certain countries. Your channel is not penalized. No strike. You can still upload, stream, and monetize other videos. Claims are routine — many creators get them regularly and simply accept them or swap the audio.

**A copyright strike** is a formal legal process. A rights holder (or their representative) submits a takedown request asserting your video infringes their copyright. YouTube removes the video, issues a strike against your channel, and restricts some features while the strike is active. This is not automated matching — it's a deliberate legal action by a human or company.

In short: a claim means the rights holder controls that video's revenue; a strike means they had the video removed and put a mark on your channel's record.

## What actually happens when you get a copyright strike?

The consequences escalate, so it's worth knowing exactly where you stand:

**First strike.** You must complete YouTube's Copyright School, a short course on copyright basics. While the strike is active, you lose access to certain features — historically including long uploads, live streaming, and custom thumbnails — so check YouTube's official help resources for the current list.

**Second strike.** No new uploads for two weeks — painful for a channel with momentum, since the [algorithm](/blog/how-youtube-algorithm-works-2026) doesn't pause.

**Third strike (within 90 days).** Your channel is terminated. All videos removed. This is the one that ends channels, and it happens to real creators every week — usually not from malice, but from accumulated carelessness: background music in vlogs, movie clips in commentary, reuploaded content they assumed was fine.

**The 90-day window matters.** Strikes expire 90 days after they're issued (assuming you completed Copyright School). Three strikes spread across a year won't kill your channel — but three within any rolling 90-day period will, so a single strike should change your behavior immediately.

Deleting the video doesn't delete the strike — it stays on your channel's record either way.

## How do you dispute a copyright claim or strike?

You have options, but each comes with risk. Here's the honest breakdown.

**Disputing a Content ID claim (low risk, usually).** In YouTube Studio, open the video's restrictions, find the claim, and select Dispute. You'll choose a reason: you own the content, you licensed it, or your use qualifies as fair use/fair dealing. Write a clear, factual explanation — not an angry paragraph. The claimant gets 30 days to respond. They can release the claim (you win), uphold it (status quo), or — importantly — escalate it into a formal takedown request, which means a strike. This escalation is rare for legitimate disputes but it's the reason you should only dispute claims you're confident about.

**Filing a counter-notification against a strike (higher stakes).** If your video was taken down and you believe it was a mistake or your use was lawful, you can file a counter-notification — a formal legal statement under penalty of perjury. The claimant then has roughly 10–14 business days to take you to court; if they don't, your video is restored. Counter-notifications can lead to actual lawsuits, so this is the point where generic advice ends and talking to a lawyer begins. Never file one casually.

**Requesting a retraction (often the best path).** If you know who issued the strike — a music label, a stock footage company — you can contact them directly, explain the situation, and ask them to retract it. Many strikes come from automated enforcement partners who will retract if you remove the offending content or show a license. Polite, prompt outreach resolves a surprising number of strikes.

**When to just accept it.** If the claim or strike is valid — you used a song you didn't license, a clip you don't own — the fastest, cheapest resolution is acceptance. For a claim: let them monetize it, or replace the audio with YouTube's free audio library tracks. For a strike: complete Copyright School, wait out the 90 days, and fix your workflow so it doesn't repeat.

## What counts as fair use on YouTube (and what doesn't)?

Fair use is the most misunderstood concept in creator copyright. It's not a rule — it's a legal defense, decided case by case in court, based on four factors: the purpose of your use (commentary, criticism, parody, and education weigh in your favor), the nature of the original work, how much you used, and whether your use harms the original's market.

**What this means in practice:**

- Reaction videos where you pause every 20 seconds to add genuine commentary are *more likely* defensible than unedited reuploads — but "more likely" isn't "safe." Rights holders still issue takedowns against commentary channels, and then you're in dispute territory.
- Using a movie clip to illustrate a point in a video essay is stronger fair-use ground than using the same clip as background decoration.

**Myths that get channels killed:**

- "Under 10/30 seconds is fine." No minimum safe duration exists.
- "I gave credit, so it's fine." Credit is not permission.
- "It's not monetized, so it's fine." Non-commercial use is only one factor, and YouTube still processes takedowns on unmonetized videos.
- "Everyone else does it." Enforcement is inconsistent — some rights holders ignore small channels for years, then sweep them all at once. Past luck is not a license.
- "I bought it on iTunes / it's on Spotify." Owning a copy is not owning the rights to use it in your content.

Fair use is a shield you raise in a dispute — not a permission slip you flash in advance.

## How do you stay safe from copyright strikes?

Prevention is dramatically cheaper than disputes. Build these habits into your workflow:

**1. Default to content you fully own.** Your footage, your voiceover, your music (or properly licensed music). Every borrowed element is a risk you're choosing to carry. Our [YouTube Upload Checklist](/tools/youtube-upload-checklist) includes a rights review step — run it before every publish, not after a strike.

**2. Use YouTube's Audio Library and licensed sources properly.** YouTube's free Audio Library is the safest music source on the platform — but read each track's terms, since some require attribution. If you buy licenses from stock music sites, keep the license documents; you'll need them if a claim arrives. Screenshot the license terms at purchase time, because terms pages change.

**3. Be careful with "royalty-free" and Creative Commons.** Royalty-free doesn't mean restriction-free — many tracks prohibit commercial use or require attribution. Creative Commons licenses vary by type, and some creators have had CC-licensed work claimed by third parties fraudulently. Verify the actual license, and prefer well-established libraries.

**4. Check every video before it goes public.** Upload as unlisted or private first and let Content ID scan it. If a claim appears, you can swap the audio or trim the section *before* the video goes live and starts earning views. This one habit prevents the majority of surprise claims. The [copyright music guide](/blog/youtube-copyright-music-guide) walks through exactly how to read those pre-publish claim notices.

**5. Treat client and sponsored content with extra care.** If a brand sends you footage or music to include, get written confirmation they hold the rights. Creators get struck over sponsor-provided assets more often than you'd think — the brand licensed it for *their* use, not yours.

**6. Keep records.** Licenses, permissions emails, receipts — organized in one folder. When a claim hits, the creator who can produce a license in five minutes wins the dispute; the creator who "thinks they bought it somewhere" loses weeks.

## What should you do the moment you get a strike?

Don't panic, don't ignore it, and don't immediately file an angry counter-notification. Work the checklist:

1. **Read the takedown notice carefully.** It identifies the claimant and the specific content. Half of panic comes from not reading.
2. **Assess honestly: was the use infringing?** If yes, accept it, complete Copyright School, and audit your other videos for the same issue — repeat strikes from the same habit are how channels die.
3. **If you have a license or clear rights, gather proof** and either dispute through YouTube Studio or contact the claimant for a retraction. Licensed music claimed by an overzealous enforcement bot is common and usually resolved quickly with documentation.
4. **Audit your catalog.** Search your other uploads for the same song, clip, or footage source. One strike is a warning; the second strike from the same mistake is negligence.
5. **Tighten your workflow** using the prevention habits above. The strike expires in 90 days — make sure it's your last one.

Copyright compliance is also a monetization issue: active strikes bring feature restrictions, and repeated infringement can cost you [Partner Program standing](/blog/youtube-monetization-requirements-explained). The creators who last aren't the ones who never err — they're the ones who build systems so mistakes don't repeat. Start with the [upload checklist](/tools/youtube-upload-checklist) and keep your licenses filed.
