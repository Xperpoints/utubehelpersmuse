## Quick answer
You can upload videos with copyrighted music, but YouTube's Content ID system will usually detect it, and the rights holder — not you — decides whether they take the revenue, block the video, or leave it alone. Crediting the artist in your description does not protect you. The safe path is licensed music, written permission, or YouTube's free Audio Library.

## How Content ID actually works
Every video you upload is scanned against a database of reference files submitted by rights holders — songs, melodies, and even short audio fingerprints. When your video's audio matches, the rights holder's pre-set policy applies automatically. Most choose to monetize your video (they get the ad revenue), some choose to track it (they collect viewing data), and some choose to block it in certain countries or worldwide.

This happens fast. Claims typically appear within minutes to a few hours of upload, which is why experienced creators upload as unlisted or private first, check for claims, and only publish once the video is clean. Our [copyright strikes guide](/blog/youtube-copyright-strikes-explained) explains what to do when a claim escalates further.

## Claims vs. strikes: the crucial difference
A Content ID claim is not a punishment — it is an automated business decision by the rights holder. Your channel is not penalized, and most claims never become strikes. You can often resolve them by swapping the audio with YouTube's built-in editor or trimming the claimed section.

A copyright strike is completely different. It is a formal legal takedown request under copyright law. Three strikes within 90 days and YouTube terminates your channel, deleting all your videos. Strikes come with real consequences from the first one: you lose access to features like live streaming and custom thumbnails while a strike is active. Treat claims as routine paperwork and strikes as emergencies.

## The myths that get channels in trouble
**"If I credit the artist, it's fine."** False. Attribution is not a license. Content ID does not read your description, and no court treats a credit line as permission.

**"Short clips under 30 seconds are safe."** There is no magic duration that makes infringement legal. Content ID can flag a few seconds of a recognizable song, and rights holders routinely claim short uses.

**"Remixes and covers are original."** A remix or cover of a copyrighted song still uses the underlying composition, which is itself copyrighted. Many cover artists get claimed or need licenses for the composition.

**"No one will notice my small channel."** Content ID is automated and does not care about your subscriber count. Small channels get claimed every day.

**"Fair use protects me."** Fair use is a legal defense, not a YouTube feature. It applies to commentary, criticism, and parody in specific circumstances — not to using a full song as background music. Claiming fair use on a Content ID dispute without understanding it is one of the fastest ways to turn a claim into a strike.

## Where can you find music that's safe for YouTube?

The simplest option is YouTube's Audio Library, built into YouTube Studio — thousands of tracks and sound effects pre-cleared for monetized videos. Third-party royalty-free libraries are the next step up in quality, usually on subscription or per-track licenses. Always read the license terms (some require attribution, some forbid use in ads or client work) and keep your license confirmations saved so you can dispute a false claim in minutes instead of days.

## What to do when you get a claim
First, do not panic. Open YouTube Studio, find the video, and read exactly what the claim says: which song, which section, and what action the rights holder chose. If they are simply monetizing, you have a genuine choice to make — many creators accept revenue-sharing on a video that would not exist without that song.

If you want the revenue back, your options are: replace the song with a track from the Audio Library using YouTube's editor, mute or trim the claimed section, or dispute the claim if you hold a license. Only dispute when you can prove your right to use the music. A dispute the claimant rejects can escalate into a takedown notice — and that is a strike. When in doubt, swap the audio. It takes five minutes and the risk disappears.

## Cover songs, remixes, and mashups: where the lines are
Covers deserve their own section because they confuse everyone. When you record your own performance of someone else's song, you own your recording — but the underlying composition (melody and lyrics) still belongs to its writers. Content ID often flags covers, and many publishers monetize them automatically. Some publishers allow covers and take the revenue; a few block them.

YouTube has built some infrastructure for this — eligible covers can share revenue with rights holders through YouTube's systems — but policies vary by publisher and region. If covers are central to your channel, research the specific publishers' stances before you build your content plan around them, and keep records of every permission or license. The [copyright strikes guide](/blog/youtube-copyright-strikes-explained) covers what happens when a cover dispute escalates.

## Using popular music on purpose: the revenue-share calculation
Sometimes using a copyrighted song is a deliberate choice, not a mistake. A trending track can make a Short or a montage dramatically better, and the rights holder claiming the ad revenue might be a price worth paying. Make that decision with open eyes: estimate the video's likely revenue, assume you keep none of the ad share, and ask whether the creative upside justifies it.

For Shorts specifically, the math is gentler — Shorts revenue per view is already tiny, so giving up the ad share costs you little while the trending audio can significantly boost reach. For long-form videos in high-RPM niches, the same decision can cost real money. There is no universally right answer, only an informed one.

## Disputing a claim, step by step
If you hold a license or permission, disputing is straightforward — but follow the process carefully:

1. In YouTube Studio, open the video's copyright section and read the claim details: what content was matched, and what the claimant chose to do.
2. Click dispute and select the reason that matches your situation (license, permission, public domain, or fair use — only if you genuinely understand it).
3. Write a clear, factual explanation: name the track, state where you licensed it, and include license or order details.
4. Submit and wait. The claimant has up to 30 days to respond, though most respond faster.

During a dispute, the claimed revenue is typically held separately — neither you nor the claimant gets it until the dispute resolves. If the claimant releases the claim, you are done. If they uphold it, you can appeal, but know that a rejected appeal can become a formal takedown — a strike. Never dispute a claim you cannot document. A calm, evidence-backed dispute wins; a hopeful one backfires.

## Building a claim-proof workflow
Make music clearance part of your [upload routine](/tools/youtube-upload-checklist), not an afterthought. Decide on your music before you edit, use only sources you have licensed, upload unlisted first to catch surprises, and file every license where you can find it. Creators who follow this routine almost never deal with claims, while creators who grab "a cool song from Spotify" deal with them constantly. The music is not the hard part — the system is.
