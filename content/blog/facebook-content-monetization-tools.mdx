## Quick answer
Facebook content monetization gives creators several ways to earn directly on the platform: Stars sent by fans, in-stream ads on longer videos, ads on Reels, subscriptions, and branded content partnerships. Which tools are available to you depends on your region, your Page's standing, and Meta's current programs — so check your Professional Dashboard and Meta's official creator pages for today's eligibility rules before planning around any single tool.

## What is Facebook content monetization?
Facebook content monetization is the set of tools Meta provides for earning money directly from your content on Facebook — as opposed to earning elsewhere (YouTube ads, sponsorships, your own products) and using Facebook only for promotion.

The hub is your Professional Dashboard, where a monetization section shows which tools your profile or Page can currently use. Broadly, the money comes from three directions: fans (Stars and subscriptions), advertisers (in-stream ads and ads on Reels), and brands (branded content partnerships). Most creators end up combining at least two — fan support for community income, ads for scale.

One important mindset shift before we go further: Facebook's monetization lineup changes. Programs launch, bonus pools dry up, eligibility rules get rewritten. Treat every specific rule you read — in this article or anywhere else — as a snapshot, not a contract. The durable strategy is understanding what each tool does and how the tools fit together, then verifying today's requirements on Meta's official pages before you act on them.

## How do Facebook Stars work?
Stars are Facebook's virtual gifting system: viewers buy Stars and send them to creators during live streams, on Reels, and on eligible on-demand videos. Each Star carries a payout value set by Meta, which converts into real money for the creator once Meta's payout thresholds and schedules are met.

The mechanic is simple, but the psychology is what makes Stars work. When a viewer sends Stars during your live stream, their name appears on screen. Thank them by name, react genuinely, and you've just demonstrated to fifty lurkers that generosity gets recognized. Creators who do well with Stars treat them as a community ritual, not a tip jar: set visible goals ("200 Stars and I'll attempt the spicy noodle challenge"), celebrate milestones on stream, and remember your regulars.

Stars perform best where community is strongest — live streams and engaged Groups especially. A Page with a quiet comment section will struggle to spark gifting no matter how good the content is, which is why Stars pair naturally with formats that build real-time togetherness. ## What are ads on Facebook Reels?
Ads on Reels place advertising on or around your short-form videos, letting creators earn from their Reels' performance. It's Meta's answer to the obvious reality that short video is where the attention went — the monetization had to follow.

In practice, earnings are performance-based: Reels that hold attention and get watched repeatedly tend to earn more than ones that get swiped past. That makes the fundamentals of Reels strategy — strong hooks in the first two seconds, tight editing, original concepts — directly tied to revenue, not just vanity metrics. The editing choices that grow your audience are the same ones that grow your payout.

A word of caution born from experience: Meta has run various Reels bonus and incentive programs over the years, and programs like these can be generous, then restructured, then gone entirely. Never build your budget around a bonus. Build it around consistent output and audience growth, treat incentive payouts as windfalls, and always confirm which programs are currently active in your Professional Dashboard rather than trusting old screenshots or forum posts.

## How do in-stream ads work?
In-stream ads are the classic model: short advertisements placed before or in the middle of your longer Facebook videos, with creators earning a share of the resulting ad revenue. If you've ever watched a mid-roll ad interrupt a Facebook video, you've seen the inventory your content could be selling.

Because these ads need somewhere to live, in-stream ads favor longer videos — the kind with natural breathing points where a mid-roll doesn't feel like an ambush. Structure helps enormously here: a video with clear segments ("three mistakes, one fix each") gives you organic pause points, while a meandering ramble will bleed viewers at every break. Think in chapters even when the platform doesn't label them.

Eligibility typically involves watch-time history and follower counts, among other requirements — the exact numbers have changed before and will change again, so verify them on Meta's official pages rather than memorizing a figure from any article, including this one. Revenue itself varies with advertiser demand, viewer region, and niche: a finance video's ad inventory is simply worth more than a meme compilation's, and that's true on every platform. ## How does branded content work on Facebook?
Branded content — paid partnerships where a brand pays you to feature their product — is often the highest-earning tool available to mid-sized creators, and it doesn't depend on Meta's ad programs at all.

Facebook's infrastructure for this is the Brand Collabs Manager, which connects creators with brands looking for partners, plus branded content tagging tools that let you formally tag your paid partner on a post. Tagging matters: it's how you disclose the commercial relationship transparently, which both Meta's policies and most advertising regulations require. Undisclosed sponsorships can get content removed and accounts penalized — the disclosure isn't optional paperwork, it's the price of admission.

You don't need a massive following to start. Brands increasingly pay smaller creators with tight, trusting niche audiences because conversions beat raw reach. What you need is a track record of content in the brand's lane, a simple media kit (audience size, demographics, past results), and professional communication. Many creators land their first deal by pitching brands they already use and love — authenticity you can't fake, and outreach that costs nothing. ## What are the eligibility requirements for Facebook monetization?
Every monetization tool has its own gate, but the gates tend to share the same ingredients: your country must be eligible for the program, your Page or professional profile must meet follower and engagement thresholds, some tools require a history of watch time on your videos, your content must comply with Meta's monetization policies (original content, no engagement bait, no policy violations), and you need a completed payout account with tax information.

I'm deliberately not listing specific numbers here, and you should be suspicious of any article that does without a recent date. Meta adjusts thresholds, adds and removes eligible countries, and rewrites policy language regularly. A figure that was correct eighteen months ago can be flat wrong today, and planning your content strategy around a stale number is a good way to waste months.

The reliable move takes two minutes: open your Professional Dashboard, go to the monetization section, and read what it says about your account right now. Then cross-check the details on Meta's official creator pages. ## How do you get started with Facebook content monetization?
Start with the account foundation. If you're monetizing as an individual, switch your profile to professional mode; if you're building a brand, set up a professional Page. Either path unlocks the Professional Dashboard, which is where you'll live from here on.

Next, open the dashboard's monetization section and see which tools show as available, in progress, or locked — that list is personalized to your account and region, and it's more trustworthy than any article. Then set up payouts before you need them: add your bank details and tax information now, because there's nothing more frustrating than earning your first payout and discovering the paperwork takes weeks.

Enable tools one at a time rather than all at once. Learn how Stars behave on your live streams before you obsess over in-stream ads; get one revenue stream working before adding the next. And guard your standing like the asset it is: a single policy violation can pause monetization across tools, so keep content original, disclose partnerships, and actually read the policy updates Meta sends instead of archiving them unread.

Finally, set honest expectations. Facebook content monetization is a revenue stream, not a salary — it builds slowly, fluctuates, and works best combined with other income like brand deals or your own products. Creators who treat it as one pillar among several — similar to the fan-support models compared in our [YouTube Super Thanks guide](/blog/youtube-super-thanks-guide) — build far more durable businesses than those betting everything on a single payout type.
