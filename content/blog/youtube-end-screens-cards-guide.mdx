## Quick answer
End screens appear in the last 5-20 seconds of a video; cards pop up during it — both promote your other content. They only work if viewers are still watching, so earn the click: run the end screen over a deliberate outro, recommend the single most obvious next video, and place cards at natural pause points, not over your best moments.

## What are YouTube end screens and cards?
They're YouTube's two built-in tools for keeping a viewer inside your content instead of bouncing back to the Home feed — but they work differently.

**End screens** appear over the final 5-20 seconds of a long-form video (25+ seconds long). You can add up to four elements: a video, a playlist, a subscribe button, a channel, or an external link for eligible channels. Add them in YouTube Studio under the video's Editor tab.

**Cards** are the small "i" teaser sliding in from the top-right during the video; tapping it opens up to five cards — usually videos, playlists, or channels — at timestamps you set in the Editor tab.

Cards catch viewers mid-video when a related topic comes up; end screens catch them at the finish line when they're deciding what next. Used well, they turn single views into sessions. Used carelessly, they distract and train viewers to ignore your calls-to-action.

## Do end screens hurt audience retention?
This is the fear that stops creators from using end screens at all — and it's mostly misplaced. The end screen element itself doesn't damage retention. What damages retention is the weak outro most creators put underneath it.

Check your [audience retention graph](/blog/youtube-audience-retention-guide): the curve often dips in the final 20 seconds. That's not the end screen's fault — the content effectively ended and the last stretch is filler ("thanks for watching, don't forget to subscribe"). Viewers sense there's nothing left and leave.

The fix isn't removing the end screen; it's making the final 20 seconds worth watching. A tight recap plus a genuine bridge to the next video ("now that you can do X, the next step is Y — that's on screen now") holds attention and converts the viewers who stayed.

Cards have a subtler risk: a teaser sliding in during a key moment — a demo climax, a punchline — yanks attention away at the worst time. If your retention graph dips exactly where a teaser fired, move it.

## When should you use end screens?
On nearly every long-form video. Someone who watched to the end gave you the strongest intent signal available — not offering a next step wastes it. Exceptions: videos under a minute, videos designed to end abruptly for effect, and videos where almost nobody reaches the final 20 seconds (fix the ending first).

Even modest end screen performance compounds: if 5% of finishing viewers click to another video, and that video's end screen converts another 5%, you've built a session chain the recommendation systems notice — one of the quiet levers behind [how the YouTube algorithm works](/blog/how-youtube-algorithm-works-2026).

Put the end screen over a designed outro, not over content. If your final point lands at 8:40 of a 9:00 video, use 8:40-9:00 as the outro zone with clean background space so elements don't cover your face or key visuals.

## How should you design an end screen that gets clicks?
Most end screens fail from clutter and vagueness. Here's what actually converts:

**Show fewer elements.** You get four slots; use two. One "best next video" plus subscribe outperforms four competing thumbnails — every extra option splits attention, and a viewer choosing between four things often chooses none.

**Recommend the obvious sequel.** The highest-converting end screen video is the one the viewer already wants: part two, the advanced version, the video you referenced three times. Generic "watch next" with an unrelated video asks the viewer to change context and converts poorly. A well-titled [playlist](/tools/youtube-playlist-title-generator) as the second element lets binge-watchers keep going.

**Say it out loud.** A verbal call-to-action lifts clicks: "If you want the advanced version, it's on screen right now." Pointing at the element feels cheesy and works — directly invite the viewers most likely to click.

**Leave visual room.** Frame your outro off-center with a clean lower-third area so elements don't cover your face. Check it on mobile — elements render smaller there.

**Measure it.** In Analytics, go to Engagement and find the end screen element click-through rate. Many creators find 2-5% is typical and 5-10% is strong. If yours is under 2%, the problem is usually relevance (wrong next video) or the lack of a spoken CTA — fix those before redesigning graphics.

## When should you use cards instead?
Cards shine in the middle of videos, at moments where a viewer's curiosity naturally branches. The best use cases:

- **Referencing a past video.** "I did a full breakdown of this setup last month" — card to that video right there, so curious viewers get depth without you re-explaining.
- **Correcting or updating.** Made an error, or a follow-up video supersedes this one? A card at the relevant timestamp fixes it for future viewers without re-editing.
- **Bridging a lull.** During a necessarily slow section (a render, a long demo), a card to a related playlist gives restless viewers somewhere to go inside your catalog.

Use one to three cards per video — each extra teaser trains viewers to ignore them. Keep the destination tightly related to the moment: a card to your channel trailer mid-tutorial is noise; a card to the advanced tutorial is service.

Note: cards and end screens don't exist on Shorts — this playbook is for long-form uploads.

## Where should you place cards without annoying viewers?
Card placement follows three rules:

**1. Natural pause points, not peaks.** Place teasers at section transitions — after one point lands, before the next begins. Never over a demo climax, a reveal, or a punchline: if the viewer is leaning in, don't interrupt the lean.

**2. Not too early.** A teaser at 0:15 feels like an ad; at 2:30 it feels like a footnote. Hold your first card until at least 60 seconds in (later in long videos).

**3. Spaced out.** Teasers firing within a minute of each other feel spammy. Spread them across sections, and check the retention graph afterward — a dip at a teaser timestamp means wrong placement.

Don't pair a card with a verbal "link in description" for the same thing at the same moment — pick one path per call-to-action or you split the click.

## End screens vs. cards: which drives more session time?
In practice, end screens drive most in-video click-throughs because of timing: a viewer at the end is actively deciding what to watch next, and your end screen arrives at that exact moment. A viewer mid-video is still consuming, so a card is an interruption — conversion is naturally lower.

So think of the division of labor: **end screens are your primary session extender** (the obvious next video, every upload); **cards are precision tools** (the deep link at the exact relevant moment).

The real multiplier is playlists: an end screen pointing to a playlist hands autoplay a whole queue of your content, turning one click into three or four views. ## Common end screen mistakes to avoid
Run through this list against your last few uploads:

- **Four cluttered elements.** Two is the sweet spot: best-next video plus subscribe (or playlist). More choices, fewer clicks.
- **An unrelated "watch next."** The end screen video should be the obvious sequel, not your latest upload shoehorned in.
- **The end screen over filler.** If the outro is dead air, viewers leave before seeing the elements. Make the final 20 seconds earn its keep.
- **No verbal call-to-action.** Silent end screens convert far worse. Tell viewers what's on screen and why they should click.
- **Subscribe button as the only element.** A subscribe click ends the session; a video click extends it. Always lead with content.
- **No end screen at all.** The most common mistake. Every finished view without a next step is session time donated to the Home feed.
- **Never checking the numbers.** End screen CTR lives in Analytics under Engagement — review it monthly as a direct readout of how well your outros convert.

Get these right and end screens become one of the highest-leverage twenty seconds on your channel: a moment you've already earned, converting finishing viewers into the extended sessions that compound across every upload.
