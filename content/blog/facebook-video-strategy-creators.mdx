## Quick answer
A strong Facebook video strategy starts with uploading videos natively instead of sharing links, because Facebook rewards content that keeps viewers on the platform. Pair that with the right length for each format, always-on captions for silent viewers, and a mix of Reels and feed videos posted consistently.

## Should you upload natively or just share links?

This is the single most common video question creators ask, and the answer is straightforward: upload your videos directly to Facebook. When you paste a YouTube link into a Facebook post, the platform shows it as a link preview. Viewers have to click away to watch, and Facebook's distribution quietly favors content that keeps people scrolling inside its own app. Many creators find that the exact same video gets dramatically more views as a native upload than as a shared link.

That does not mean links are useless. If your main business lives on YouTube and you want to move a loyal slice of your Facebook audience there, an occasional link post is a legitimate traffic play. Just understand the trade-off: a link post will almost always reach fewer people than a native video. A practical middle ground is to upload the full video natively for reach, and add your YouTube channel link in the page description, a pinned comment, or the post text so interested viewers can find you elsewhere.

There is one more link habit worth breaking. Some creators crosspost from Instagram with a watermarked or low-resolution file. Visible watermarks, blurry footage, and letterboxed landscape clips all signal low effort, and viewers swipe away. Since retention drives distribution, anything that makes the first three seconds feel cheap quietly hurts the whole post. Upload a clean file made for Facebook, even if it is the same footage you used elsewhere.

## What video length actually works best on Facebook?

There is no single magic number, because Facebook is really several video products wearing one name. The right length depends on which surface you are posting to and what you want the video to do.

**Under 60 seconds: Reels.** This is the format built for discovery. Short, punchy Reels are what Facebook pushes to non-followers, so this is where new audience growth comes from. Hooks matter more here than polish. If your niche can be compressed into a tip, a reaction, or a satisfying before-and-after, put it in a Reel. Our full breakdown of the format lives in the [Facebook Reels strategy guide](/blog/facebook-reels-strategy-2026).

**1 to 3 minutes: feed videos.** This is the sweet spot for everyday page content — tutorials, commentary, mini-vlogs, and story-driven clips. Long enough to deliver real value, short enough that busy scrollers finish. Feed videos also give you room for a text caption that adds context, which helps both viewers and search.

**3 minutes and up: Watch and monetization.** Longer videos qualify for in-stream ads where available, and they let you go deeper for an established audience. Do not stretch a 90-second idea into four minutes just to chase ad breaks, though. Retention is the metric that matters; a 6-minute video that half your audience finishes beats a 12-minute one everyone abandons. If monetization is your goal, skim the [content monetization tools overview](/blog/facebook-content-monetization-tools) and check the [Facebook monetization requirements](/blog/facebook-monetization-requirements) before you build your format around ad revenue.

One practical rule ties these together: cut ruthlessly. Open every video by showing or promising the payoff in the first three seconds, then deliver it as fast as you honestly can. Creators who rewatch their own drafts at double speed and delete anything that drags usually see retention climb.

## Do captions really matter on Facebook?

Yes, more than on almost any other platform. A big share of Facebook video views happen with the sound off — people scrolling on the bus, in the office, or in bed next to someone sleeping. If your video makes no sense without audio, those viewers are gone within two seconds, and that early drop-off tells the algorithm the video is not worth pushing.

You have two good options. The simplest is uploading an SRT caption file or using Facebook's auto-captions, then fixing the mistakes (auto-captions are decent but they will butcher names, slang, and niche terms). The better option for Reels and short feed videos is burned-in captions — large, styled text baked into the video itself. Styled captions double as a visual design element, they work even when platforms strip metadata, and they let you emphasize key words with color and timing.

A few caption details that separate amateur from professional: keep each caption line to a few words so eyes do not have to travel, sync text tightly to speech so viewers do not read ahead, and never cover the caption area with stickers or watermarks. If you talk to the camera, position yourself slightly above center so captions can sit in the lower third without hiding your face. And always watch your draft once with the sound fully off. If you cannot follow it, your silent audience cannot either.

Captions also help your videos travel. Facebook indexes caption text, which means a video with accurate captions has a better chance of showing up when someone searches for your topic. It is a small discoverability bonus on top of the retention benefit.

## Reels vs. regular uploads: where should your effort go?

Think of Reels and feed videos as two different jobs. Reels are the scout: they reach people who do not follow you and turn strangers into followers. Feed videos are the relationship: they go deeper for people who already chose you, and they earn the comments, shares, and saves that build a loyal page.

Most creators do best with a mix, weighted toward whichever job they need most. If your page is new and discovery is the bottleneck, lean into Reels — four or five a week — with one longer feed video to give new followers something to binge. If your page already has an audience but engagement is flat, flip it: two or three deeper feed videos a week plus a couple of Reels to keep discovery alive.

Format choices matter too. Reels should be vertical (9:16), fill the whole phone screen, and grab attention instantly. Feed videos can be vertical or square (1:1); square takes up more space in the scrolling feed than landscape and tends to hold attention better on desktop. Never upload horizontal video with big black bars if you can reframe it — that wasted screen space is viewers you paid for in effort and never got.

Live video is a third format worth a slot in your rotation. Facebook notifies followers when you go live, and live sessions typically generate far more comments than pre-recorded uploads. ## A simple weekly Facebook video plan

Strategy means nothing without a schedule you can actually keep. Here is a starter plan for a creator posting video as their main format:

- **Monday:** one Reel — a quick tip, a hot take, or a behind-the-scenes moment. Keep it under 45 seconds.
- **Wednesday:** one feed video, 1 to 3 minutes — your meatiest value post of the week. A mini-tutorial or story works well.
- **Friday:** one Reel — lighter content. A trend, a blooper, or a question that invites comments.
- **Once or twice a month:** one longer video (5+ minutes) or a live session for your core audience.

That is three videos a week, which is enough to learn what works without burning out. After a month, check your insights: which videos kept people watching past the 3-second mark, and which earned shares? Shares are the strongest signal on Facebook — a share puts your video in front of a whole new circle. Double down on whatever gets shared, not just whatever gets views. For timing your posts, see the guide on the [best time to post on Facebook](/blog/best-time-to-post-on-facebook).

Batch your production to make this sustainable. Film four Reels in one sitting, edit them across the week, and you have solved the hardest part of consistency: starting.

## Small details that quietly boost video reach

The big levers are native uploads, length, and captions. These smaller ones add up:

- **The first frame matters.** Facebook often shows a thumbnail before the video plays. Pick a frame with a face, bold text, or visual curiosity — not a blurry mid-blink frame.
- **Write a real post caption.** The text above your video is not decoration. One or two sentences that set up the payoff ("The pricing mistake that cost me my first client") give scrollers a reason to stop. The [post ideas generator](/tools/facebook-post-ideas) can help when you are stuck for angles.
- **Ask for the right action.** "Share this with someone who needs it" outperforms "like and follow" because shares drive new reach. Use it only when the video genuinely deserves sharing.
- **Pin your best video.** A pinned post is your page's trailer. New visitors decide in seconds whether to follow; make sure your best 60 seconds is what they see first.
- **Reply to early comments fast.** The first hour of engagement shapes distribution. Answer questions, ask follow-ups, and keep the conversation going — this feeds directly into how the algorithm reads your post, as explained in the [page growth guide](/blog/facebook-page-growth-guide).

None of these replace good content, but good content with sloppy packaging loses to good content with sharp packaging every time. Treat the upload as part of the creative work: thumbnail frame, caption text, captions file, and a pinned comment with your link. Get those right and the same video reaches more people with no extra filming.
