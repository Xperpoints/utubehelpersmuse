## Quick answer
The best time to post on Facebook for most Pages falls on weekday late mornings through early afternoons — roughly 9 a.m. to 3 p.m. in your audience's timezone — when people check their feeds during breaks and commutes. But that's a starting point, not a rule: your audience's habits matter more than any global average. The real answer comes from your Page Insights, which show exactly when your followers are online. Test the general windows, measure your results, and shift toward the times that earn you the most engagement. And if your Page itself isn't discoverable yet, start with our [Facebook Page SEO guide](/blog/facebook-page-seo-guide) before optimizing timing.

## What is the best time to post on Facebook, generally speaking?
If you want a starting point backed by years of aggregated data, here is the honest summary: weekday late mornings through early afternoons tend to perform best for the average Page. People scroll Facebook during lunch breaks, mid-morning pauses, and the afternoon slump — so posts published between about 9 a.m. and 3 p.m., Tuesday through Thursday, usually get the strongest early traction.

Weekends are mixed. Saturday mornings can work well for lifestyle, food, and entertainment content, while Sunday evenings see a bump as people wind down before the work week. Early mornings (before 7 a.m.) and late nights (after 10 p.m.) are generally the weakest slots, simply because fewer people are awake and scrolling.

Two caveats matter more than the numbers themselves. First, these are averages across millions of Pages — your niche may behave differently. A Page for night-shift workers or late-night gamers will have a completely different rhythm than a Page for office professionals. Second, time zones: "9 a.m." means 9 a.m. where your audience lives, not where you live. A creator in Karachi with a US audience who posts at 9 a.m. PKT is publishing around midnight Eastern — dead air. Always anchor timing to your audience's timezone, which Page Insights will reveal.

## Why is there no universal best time to post on Facebook?
Facebook's feed doesn't show posts chronologically — it ranks them. When you publish, Facebook shows your post to a small test audience first and watches what happens: do they stop scrolling, react, comment, share? Strong early engagement signals the algorithm to show it to more people. Posting when your specific followers are online maximizes that crucial first hour.

This is why copying someone else's "perfect posting time" often disappoints. A food blogger whose audience scrolls at lunch will flop posting at 8 p.m.; a gaming Page whose fans are night owls will flop posting at 9 a.m. The algorithm rewards relevance and recency together — your post needs to be fresh at the moment your people open the app.

Competition matters too. The most popular posting windows are also the most crowded. Sometimes a slightly off-peak slot — say 7 a.m. or 4 p.m. — outperforms the noon rush because your post faces less competition in the feed. The only way to know is to test, which brings us to the method that actually works.

## How do you find YOUR best time using Page Insights?
Forget global studies for a moment — Facebook hands you the answer for your specific audience, free, inside Page Insights. Here is the method, step by step:

**Step 1: Find the online-followers chart.** Open Meta Business Suite on desktop, go to Insights, then the audience section. Look for the chart showing when your followers are online, broken down by day of week and hour. This is the single most valuable timing data you own — it's your audience, not an average.

**Step 2: Identify your top 3–5 peak windows.** Note both the days and the hours. You'll usually see clear humps — maybe Wednesday 11 a.m. to 1 p.m. and Sunday 8 to 10 p.m. Write them down.

**Step 3: Schedule posts 30–60 minutes before each peak, not during it.** Facebook needs a little lead time to start distributing your post, so publishing just ahead of the wave means your content is fresh and gathering early engagement right as the crowd arrives.

**Step 4: Run a four-week test.** Post similar-quality content across your candidate windows and track reach and engagement per post. Keep a simple spreadsheet: date, time, format, reach, engagement rate. After a month, patterns emerge that no generic guide could give you. Our [Facebook engagement rate calculator](/tools/facebook-engagement-rate-calculator) makes the engagement math quick.

**Step 5: Revisit quarterly.** Audiences shift — school terms start, seasons change, your follower mix evolves. What worked in summer may fade by winter. A quick Insights check every few months keeps your timing honest.

One more tip: separate your analysis by content type. Your audience might watch Reels at night but read long posts at lunch. Insights won't split this for you automatically, so tag your test posts by format in your spreadsheet.

## Should timing differ by content type?
Yes — format and timing interact, and smart creators plan for it:

- **Reels and short video** do well in evening leisure hours (roughly 7–10 p.m.), when people are relaxed and willing to watch. Discovery-driven formats benefit from high-traffic windows.
- **Link posts and articles** perform better during workday breaks, when people have a few minutes to click through and read. Mid-morning and lunch are your friends here.
- **Live streams** need advance notice more than perfect timing. Promote the stream 24–48 hours ahead, then go live when your Insights show strong online presence. Consistency — same day, same time weekly — builds a habit audience that shows up regardless of the slot.
- **Stories** are forgiving. They sit at the top of the app for 24 hours, so exact timing matters less. Morning Stories catch the day's first scroll; evening Stories catch the wind-down.

If you're repurposing the same content across formats, stagger the timing rather than posting everything at once. Our guide to [repurposing content across platforms](/blog/how-to-repurpose-content-across-platforms) covers how to sequence this without burning out your audience.

One underused timing lever is the pinned post. Pin your most important post of the week — a new video, an offer, an announcement — to the top of your Page so it captures traffic regardless of when people visit. A pinned post effectively decouples that one piece of content from the timing game entirely, giving slow-burn content a full week to accumulate engagement.

## How often should you post on Facebook?
Timing means little without consistency. For most Pages, one quality post per day is the sweet spot — enough to stay visible without overwhelming followers or diluting your average engagement. New or small Pages can start with four to five posts per week and scale up as the content pipeline allows.

Quality beats quantity decisively. Facebook's algorithm tracks how people respond to your Page over time; a Page that posts three mediocre updates daily trains its audience to scroll past, which drags down the reach of everything — including the good stuff. One strong post that earns comments will outperform five filler posts every time.

Build a sustainable rhythm with a [content calendar](/blog/content-calendar-for-creators) rather than posting on impulse. Batch-create a week's posts in one sitting, schedule them at your tested windows, and spend the freed-up time engaging with comments — replies in the first hour after posting are one of the simplest reach boosters available. For fresh material when the well runs dry, the [Facebook post ideas generator](/tools/facebook-post-ideas) can spark a week's worth of angles in minutes.

And if you manage multiple platforms, compare notes: your [best time to post on Instagram](/blog/best-time-to-post-on-instagram) may differ from Facebook's, since the audiences skew differently even when the followers overlap. Test each platform on its own terms instead of copy-pasting one schedule everywhere.
