## Quick answer

An optimized YouTube description puts keywords in the first two to three lines, summarizes the video for viewers and search, and adds timestamps, links, and hashtags. Use a consistent template so every upload takes ten minutes, not an hour.

Your video description does two jobs at once: it tells YouTube's algorithm what your video is about, and it converts casual viewers into subscribers, customers, or followers. Most creators fail at both because they write descriptions as an afterthought — two lazy sentences typed in the upload rush.

A good description takes ten minutes with a template. Here's the exact structure I recommend, why each section exists, and a fully annotated example you can adapt.

## Do YouTube descriptions still matter?

Yes — YouTube has said directly that descriptions help its systems understand video content. They boost search visibility for your target query and long-tail searches, the first ~150 characters act as a second headline in search results, they're the only place on the watch page for clickable links and timestamps, and the text helps YouTube relate your video to suggested content. A great description won't save a video nobody clicks on — but among two similar videos, the better description consistently wins.

YouTube has said directly that descriptions help its systems understand video content. But the real power is compound:

- **Search visibility:** keyword-rich descriptions help you rank for your target query and related long-tail searches.
- **Click-through from search:** roughly the first 150 characters appear under your title in search results before truncation. That's a second headline most creators waste.
- **Conversion:** it's the only place on the watch page where you can place clickable links, timestamps, and calls to action.
- **Suggested video context:** YouTube uses description text to understand which videos are topically related to yours.

The honest caveat: a great description won't save a video nobody clicks on. But among two similar videos, the one with the better description consistently wins the ranking — this plays out repeatedly.

## The Anatomy of a High-Performing Description

### Section 1: The Hook (First 1-2 Sentences, ~150 Characters)

This is the most important real estate in your description. It appears in search results and above the "show more" fold. It must contain your primary keyword and give a searcher a reason to click.

Weak: "Hey everyone! Welcome back to the channel. Today I have a super exciting video for you!"

That tells YouTube nothing and tells the searcher nothing. Compare:

Strong: "This Sony WH-1000XM5 review tests sound quality, noise cancelling, and comfort after 60 days of daily use — plus how it compares to the Bose QC Ultra at the same price."

Keyword in the first five words. Specific claims. A reason to click. Write this section like a second title.

### Section 2: The Summary (100-200 Words)

Expand on the hook with 2-4 sentences that naturally include your primary keyword once more plus 2-3 related terms. Describe what the viewer will learn, who the video is for, and what makes your take different.

This is where most creators either write nothing or write a novel. Aim for a short paragraph that a busy person can skim in ten seconds. Think of it as the back-cover blurb of your video.

### Section 3: Timestamps / Chapters

For any video over five minutes, add timestamps. Format them exactly like this:

00:00 Intro
02:15 Unboxing and first impressions
05:40 Sound quality test
09:20 Noise cancelling comparison
13:05 Comfort after long sessions
16:30 Final verdict: should you buy it?

YouTube converts these into clickable chapters automatically. They improve retention (viewers navigate instead of leaving), help you win "key moments" in Google search, and give the algorithm a structured map of your content. Our [YouTube Timestamp Link Generator](/tools/youtube-timestamp-link-generator) formats these cleanly if you're building them from scratch.

### Section 4: Links and Resources

List the products, tools, or sources mentioned in the video. Keep it scannable — one link per line with a short label. This section builds trust because it proves you actually used what you're talking about.

Example:
- Headphones reviewed: [link]
- My full written review: [link]
- Comparison spreadsheet: [link]

### Section 5: Call to Action

One clear CTA, not five. "Subscribe for weekly honest tech reviews — no sponsorships, no fluff." Tell them what they'll get and how often. A specific promise ("weekly honest tech reviews") converts far better than a generic "please subscribe."

### Section 6: Social Links and Standard Footer

Your other channels, website, and business contact. This can be identical across videos — it's the one section where a template is not just fine but expected. Add an affiliate disclosure here if you use affiliate links ("As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases") — it's legally required in many countries and builds trust.

### Section 7: Hashtags (3-5)

End with a few relevant hashtags. YouTube displays the first three above your title. Keep them specific to the video: #SonyWH1000XM5 #HeadphoneReview #TechReview.

## Common Description Mistakes

**Burying the keyword.** If your target keyword first appears in paragraph three, you've wasted the highest-weight positions. Keyword in sentence one, related terms in sentences two through four.

**The novel-length description.** Nobody reads 1,000 words under a video. If your description needs scrolling to reach the timestamps, trim it. Dense doesn't mean thorough — it means unread.

**No timestamps on long videos.** This is the single most common miss I see on 15+ minute videos. Chapters take two minutes to add and measurably improve both retention and Google key-moments eligibility.

**Five competing CTAs.** "Subscribe! Follow me on Instagram! Join my Discord! Buy my course! Check out my Patreon!" — when everything is urgent, nothing is. Pick the one action that matters most for this video.

**Copy-pasting the identical description on every video.** A shared footer is fine. But when ten videos share the same 300-word block, YouTube has less unique text to understand each video, and viewers notice the laziness. Customize the hook and summary every time.
