## Quick answer

Shorts pay roughly $0.05-$0.15 RPM while long-form pays $2-$10+, but Shorts deliver discovery speed long-form cannot match. Most creators do best with a hybrid: Shorts for growth, long-form for revenue and depth.

Every creator asks this question eventually: should I make Shorts or long-form videos? You'll hear confident answers in both directions, usually from people selling a course about whichever one they picked.

Here's the honest version: Shorts and long-form are completely different products with different economics. Neither is "better." They do different jobs, and once you understand the actual math, the right strategy for your channel becomes obvious.

## The money math nobody wants to hear

Let's start with the number that settles most debates. YouTube pays creators through RPM — revenue per 1,000 views. For long-form videos, RPM typically lands between $2 and $10 depending on your niche, with finance and tech channels reaching $15 to $25.

For Shorts, RPM typically sits between **$0.03 and $0.10** for most creators — low single-digit cents. Read that again. It's not a typo.

Run the math on one million views:

- **1,000,000 Shorts views** at a $0.10 RPM = **about $100**
- **1,000,000 long-form views** at a $5 RPM = **about $5,000**

That is a 50x gap for the same view count. A Short that "goes viral" with 2 million views might earn you $200 — less than a single decent long-form video with 40,000 views in a good niche.

This doesn't mean Shorts are worthless. It means you should never confuse Shorts views with real revenue. If your goal is to make money from ads, long-form wins by a landslide and it's not close. But money isn't the only goal, which is where this gets interesting.

## What Shorts are actually good at

**Discovery at a speed long-form can't match.** YouTube pushes Shorts aggressively to new viewers. A Short from a channel with zero subscribers can hit 100,000 views in a day if the hook lands. A long-form video from the same channel would be lucky to get 200 views in its first week. If you're starting from nothing, Shorts are the fastest way to find out whether anyone cares about what you make.

**Cheap experimentation.** A Short takes an hour to film and edit. A long-form video takes a day or more. That means you can test ten ideas as Shorts in the time it takes to produce one long video, see which topics resonate, then expand the winners into full videos. This is the smartest use of Shorts that almost nobody talks about: they're a testing lab.

**Subscriber growth.** Shorts convert viewers to subscribers efficiently because the ask is tiny — someone enjoyed 30 seconds and taps subscribe on impulse. You'll grow your subscriber number fast.

## What Shorts are bad at

**Revenue**, as we covered. But the subtler problems matter more.

**Subscriber quality is low.** This is the uncomfortable truth Shorts evangelists skip. A subscriber gained from a 20-second comedy clip has almost no relationship with you. When you publish a 12-minute video, most of them won't click — they subscribed for quick hits, not for you. Crossover rates from Shorts audiences to long-form viewership commonly sit under 10%. A channel with 500K subscribers built mostly from Shorts can easily get fewer views on a long-form upload than a channel with 20K loyal long-form subscribers.

**No compounding search traffic.** Long-form videos rank in search and get suggested for years. A good tutorial can earn views every single day for five years straight. Shorts have a shelf life measured in days — the feed moves on. You're on a treadmill: stop posting and the views stop.

**Shallow audience relationship.** Nobody becomes a true fan from a 30-second clip. Fans — the people who buy your products, join your memberships, show up to everything — come from spending real time with you. That only happens in long-form.

## What long-form is actually good at

**Real money.** Covered above, but it extends beyond ads. Sponsors pay for long-form integrations — a 60-second spot inside a 12-minute video — and almost never for Shorts. A mid-size long-form channel can earn more from two sponsorships a month than from all its ad revenue combined.

**A loyal audience.** Someone who watches you for 12 minutes straight, week after week, is invested. These are the viewers who comment thoughtfully, share your videos, and stick around for years. Subscriber counts lie; watch time tells the truth.

**Evergreen compounding.** A well-optimized tutorial or review keeps earning views from search indefinitely. Each video becomes a small asset that pays you while you sleep. After two years of consistent publishing, a long-form channel often gets most of its views from its back catalog, not new uploads.

## What long-form is bad at

**Slow growth.** The first 6 to 12 months of a long-form channel are brutal. You'll publish videos that get 40 views and wonder why you bother. The algorithm needs data to learn who wants your content, and that data accumulates slowly.

**High effort per video.** Research, scripting, filming, editing, thumbnail, packaging — a good long-form video is 10 to 20 hours of work. That's a heavy bet to place when you're unsure the topic will land.

**Harder to experiment.** Because each video costs so much, creators get conservative. They keep making the same video because it worked once, and the channel slowly goes stale.

## So which should you choose?

It depends on what you actually want. Be honest with yourself:

- **Your goal is ad revenue and a sustainable business:** long-form, no question. Build a library of search-driven videos in a monetizable niche.
- **Your goal is audience growth as fast as possible:** Shorts, with the understanding that you're building a wide, shallow audience.
- **Your goal is both:** the hybrid strategy below, which is what I'd recommend to almost everyone.

## The hybrid strategy that actually works

Here's the setup I recommend: publish **2 to 3 Shorts per week and 1 long-form video per week**. But the key is that they feed each other deliberately, not randomly.

Use Shorts as the top of your funnel. Take the strongest 30 seconds of your long-form video — the most surprising moment, the best tip, the funniest bit — and post it as a Short. Pin a comment linking to the full video. Some viewers will click through. Even at a 3 to 5% click-through rate, a Short with 200,000 views sends 6,000 to 10,000 viewers to your long-form video. That's more than most small channels get from browse.

Use Shorts as your testing lab. Before committing a week to a long-form video, post the core idea as a Short. If it flops with your audience, you just saved yourself 15 hours. If it takes off, you already know the long-form version has demand.

And keep the formats honest with each other. Don't expect your Shorts audience to magically become long-form viewers. Track them separately in your analytics. A healthy hybrid channel might get 90% of its views from Shorts and 90% of its revenue from long-form — and that's completely fine, because each format is doing its job.

Let me sketch realistic six-month math for a cooking channel doing this. The Shorts pull 2 million total views — about $200 in ad revenue, but 25,000 new subscribers. The 24 long-form videos average 15,000 views each — 360,000 views at a $6 food-niche RPM, about $2,160. The Shorts didn't pay the bills. But without them, those long-form videos might have averaged 3,000 views instead of 15,000, because the Shorts-fed subscribers gave every upload an initial audience that signaled value to the algorithm. That's the synergy: Shorts supply the spark, long-form supplies the fire and the income.

## One more honest caveat

Don't count Shorts views when you assess your channel's health. It's common to see creators celebrate 10 million Shorts views while their long-form videos get only a few hundred views each — and they can't figure out why they feel stuck. Vanity metrics are called vanity metrics for a reason. Track watch hours, returning viewers, and revenue per upload — those are the numbers that tell you whether you're building something real.

Shorts are a tool. Long-form is a tool. Use each one for what it's actually good at, and stop expecting either to do the other's job.
