## Quick answer
A WhatsApp broadcast list sends your message to up to 256 contacts privately, like a personal DM to each person, while a WhatsApp group puts everyone in one shared chat where members can see and reply to each other. Use broadcasts for one-way updates and offers to an audience that opted in; use groups for community discussion, feedback, and member-to-member connection.

## What is a WhatsApp broadcast list?
A broadcast list is a saved list of contacts you message at once. When you send, each recipient gets the message as if you had typed it only to them. There is no shared thread, no "seen by 40 people" counter, and no reply-all chaos. Replies come back to you privately.

The mechanics matter for creators. The list caps at 256 contacts, and delivery has a catch: only contacts who have saved your phone number in their address book receive your broadcasts. Someone who tapped your link, asked you to add them, but never saved your number will silently never get your messages. That is the single most common reason broadcasts "stop working," and it is a consent feature, not a bug.

Broadcasts are also quiet by design. No notifications fire for anyone except each individual recipient receiving a normal message. Nobody can see the list, so subscriber identities stay private — a real advantage when your audience values discretion.

## What is a WhatsApp group?
A group is a shared chat room. Everyone sees the member list, everyone's messages, and every reply. Groups now support up to 1,024 members, which makes them the larger-format option. Admins can restrict who sends messages, pin announcements, and manage join approvals through invite links.

The social dynamic is the opposite of a broadcast. Members talk to each other, not just to you. That is powerful when it works — a busy group becomes a community with its own energy — and exhausting when it doesn't, because off-topic chatter, arguments, and notification overload are your responsibility to manage.

## WhatsApp broadcast vs group: the key differences
The practical comparison comes down to a few questions:

- **Visibility:** Broadcasts are invisible to recipients; groups expose every member to every other member.
- **Conversation:** Broadcasts are one-to-many with private replies; groups are many-to-many.
- **Size:** 256 contacts per broadcast list vs up to 1,024 members per group.
- **Delivery requirement:** Broadcasts require recipients to have your number saved; groups only require the invite link or an admin add.
- **Management load:** Broadcasts are nearly zero-maintenance once the list exists; groups need moderation, rules, and sometimes co-admins.
- **Engagement style:** Broadcasts feel personal and direct; groups feel social and communal.

There is also a third option worth knowing: [WhatsApp Channels](/blog/whatsapp-channels-growth-guide), which are built for public, one-way updates with unlimited followers. Think of Channels as the public announcement layer, broadcasts as the personal newsletter layer, and groups as the community living room.

## When to use a WhatsApp broadcast list
Broadcasts fit moments where the message is about you talking to each person individually. Real use cases for creators:

- **New content alerts.** A short message — "New video is live: [title]" — sent the moment you publish. Because it lands like a DM, open rates tend to be high, often much higher than email for audiences that live on WhatsApp.
- **Exclusive drops and early access.** Presale links, unlisted videos, or early-bird slots feel genuinely exclusive in a broadcast because nobody else can see the list.
- **Personal updates and reminders.** Webinar starting in 10 minutes, a live Q&A, a poll you want votes on — anything time-sensitive and personal.
- **Segmented offers.** Keep separate lists — buyers vs free subscribers, one language vs another — and message each with relevant content instead of blasting everyone with everything.

Broadcasts fail when you treat them like a spam cannon. Keep frequency modest — most creators do well with one to four broadcasts a week — and make every message worth opening. If someone stops replying or engaging, that is feedback.

## When to use a WhatsApp group
Groups fit when the value comes from members interacting with each other:

- **Community hubs.** A fan group, a course cohort, or a niche community (fitness challengers, language learners, study buddies) where members share wins, ask questions, and motivate each other.
- **Feedback and ideas.** Ask the group what video to make next, which thumbnail they prefer, or what problem they are stuck on. The discussion itself is content research.
- **Live event coordination.** Travel meetups, watch parties, local events — groups are natural coordination spaces.
- **Support communities.** If you sell a course or membership, a group gives buyers a place to get help and meet peers.

The cost is moderation. Set rules early — no spam, no off-topic forwards, stay kind — pin them, and enforce them. Many creators keep the group announcement-only most of the time and open chat on specific days, which gives community energy without 24/7 noise.

## Broadcast vs group vs channel: quick decision guide
Ask yourself three questions. First: **how big is the audience?** Under 256 and private — broadcast. Hundreds to a thousand and interactive — group. Unlimited and public — Channel. Second: **who talks?** If only you need to talk, broadcast or Channel. If members should talk to each other, group. Third: **how public?** If you want discoverability, Channels are searchable; broadcasts and groups stay invitation-only.

Many creators end up using all three in layers: a Channel for public updates, a broadcast list for the inner circle that opted in, and a group for paying members or superfans. That layering gives people a path from casual follower to core community member.

## The consent rule: opt-in or nothing
This is the part that keeps your number alive. WhatsApp bans numbers that get reported for spam, and the fastest route to a ban is adding people who never asked.

- **Get clear opt-in.** Someone should take an action that says "yes, message me" — tapping a [click-to-chat link](/tools/whatsapp-click-to-chat-link-generator) on your profile, filling a form, replying YES to a prompt. Pre-ticked boxes and scraped numbers do not count.
- **Ask them to save your number.** Since broadcasts only reach saved contacts, make this step explicit: "Tap here to chat, then save my number so you get my updates." The [click-to-chat link generator](/tools/whatsapp-click-to-chat-link-generator) gives you a clean link that opens a chat with a pre-filled message — perfect for opt-in flows.
- **Say what they'll get and how often.** "One message a week with new video alerts" sets expectations and cuts unsubscribes.
- **Offer an easy opt-out.** "Reply STOP anytime" — and honor it immediately. A clean list of engaged subscribers beats a big list of annoyed strangers.
- **Never buy or scrape numbers.** Beyond the ban risk, it poisons trust before you ever earn it.

## How to set up your first broadcast list
Open WhatsApp, tap the new chat icon, and choose "New broadcast." Select contacts from people who have opted in — remember, they must have your number saved too. Name the list something you will recognize, like "Video alerts" or "Course buyers." Before your first real message, send a short welcome broadcast that confirms what they signed up for and how to opt out.

For the message itself, formatting helps. The [WhatsApp text formatter](/tools/whatsapp-text-formatter) lets you draft bold, italic, and strikethrough text you can paste straight into WhatsApp, which makes announcements noticeably more scannable. Keep messages under a few short paragraphs, put the link early, and end with one clear action.

## How to set up a healthy group
Create the group from opted-in members or share an invite link only where your real audience hangs out — your video description, your bio, your Channel. Set the group description to state the purpose and rules. Decide early whether all members can send messages or only admins; announcement-only groups with periodic open-chat windows give you the best of both worlds. Appoint one or two trusted members as co-admins before you need them, not after a crisis.

## Common mistakes creators make
- **Adding people without asking.** The number-one ban risk and trust killer.
- **Broadcasting too often.** Daily blasts train people to mute you. Quality over quantity, always.
- **Sending the same message everywhere.** Your broadcast, group, Channel, and email should not be identical copies. Tailor the format to the channel.
- **No opt-out path.** People who cannot leave easily report instead.
- **Ignoring replies.** Broadcast replies are private gold — questions, feedback, testimonials. Answer them.
- **One giant unsegmented list.** A 200-person list where half the messages are irrelevant to each half performs worse than two focused lists.

## Measuring what works
Watch reply rates on broadcasts — replies mean the message landed. In groups, watch the ratio of meaningful messages to noise, and whether the group grows through member invites (a sign of health) or stalls. Track click-throughs on your links by comparing views or signups against list size. And periodically prune: remove numbers that never engage from broadcast lists, and remove disruptive members from groups. A smaller, engaged audience outperforms a large, silent one every time.

If you are still deciding between formats, start with a broadcast list. It is the lowest-effort way to test whether your audience wants WhatsApp updates at all, and you can invite your most engaged subscribers into a group later.
