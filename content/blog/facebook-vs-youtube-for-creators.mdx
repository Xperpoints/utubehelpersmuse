## Quick answer
There is no universal winner in the Facebook vs YouTube debate — they reward different things. Facebook delivers fast, social discovery through shares and groups, but posts fade within days. YouTube grows slower at first, then compounds: a searchable library that can send you viewers for years. Choose Facebook if your content sparks conversation, YouTube if it answers questions people search for — and many creators eventually build on both.

## How does discovery work on Facebook vs YouTube?
Discovery is where the two platforms differ most, and the difference shapes everything else about your strategy.

On Facebook, discovery is social. The feed ranks content by relationships and interactions: what your friends engage with, what groups you're in, what gets shared and reshared. A video takes off because people pass it to people — shares into groups, tags in comments, reposts by Pages. That makes Facebook explosive for content with an emotional trigger: funny, relatable, surprising, heartwarming. The trade-off is that the explosion is brief. The feed moves on, and yesterday's viral hit is buried by tomorrow morning.

On YouTube, discovery is intent-driven. YouTube doubles as a search engine — people arrive with a question and leave with your video as the answer. Suggested videos then keep them watching more. Growth is slower because you're earning one searcher at a time, but each video is a permanent asset. A tutorial can rank in search, get suggested alongside bigger channels, and collect subscribers for years with zero additional effort from you.

Consider two videos: "watch me attempt the world's spiciest ramen" and "how to fix a leaking faucet." The ramen clip is built for Facebook — shareable, emotional, dead in 48 hours. The faucet tutorial is built for YouTube — searchable, useful, still alive years later. Neither is better; they serve different audience needs. Your job is to figure out which need your content serves, then invest where that need lives.

## Which platform pays creators more reliably?
Let's be honest first: neither platform guarantees income, and earnings vary enormously by niche, region, and advertiser demand. Anyone promising you specific earnings from either platform is selling something. Treat everything below as structure, not promises.

YouTube's money comes primarily from its Partner Program, which shares ad revenue with creators on long-form videos once channels meet the program's eligibility requirements — subscriber and watch-time thresholds you'll want to verify on YouTube's official pages, since they do change. Our breakdown of [YouTube monetization requirements](/blog/youtube-monetization-requirements-explained) and what [YouTube pays per 1,000 views](/blog/how-much-youtube-pays-per-1000-views) goes deeper, but the headline is predictability: an evergreen library earns while you sleep, and revenue roughly tracks with watch time, which you can directly influence.

Facebook's money is more fragmented: Stars from fans, in-stream ads on longer videos, ads on Reels, and various bonus programs that have appeared, changed, and disappeared over the years. That last part matters — building your finances around a bonus program is risky, because bonuses sunset. We cover the full toolkit in our guide to [Facebook's content monetization tools](/blog/facebook-content-monetization-tools), with the standing advice to confirm current eligibility on Meta's official pages before planning around any single tool.

The practical takeaway: YouTube revenue tends to be steadier for creators with searchable libraries, while Facebook income can spike higher and faster around viral moments but fluctuates more. Creators who last treat both as revenue streams to diversify across — never the whole plan.

## Who actually owns your audience on each platform?
Here's the uncomfortable truth both platforms share: you don't own your audience on either one. Subscribers and followers are relationships the platform mediates. An algorithm change, a policy strike, or a compromised account can cut your reach overnight — on Facebook or YouTube equally.

That said, the shape of the relationship differs. YouTube subscribers who enable notifications get alerted to new videos, and search keeps delivering strangers who chose your topic deliberately — that intent makes YouTube audiences unusually loyal to the channel itself. Facebook followers and group members, meanwhile, encounter you socially; reach depends heavily on engagement signals, but Groups give creators something YouTube lacks: a genuine community space where members talk to each other, not just to you.

Smart creators on both platforms funnel toward assets they actually own — an email list, a website, a community off-platform. Use Facebook and YouTube as discovery engines, but make sure that every month, some fraction of that audience becomes reachable without any algorithm's permission. Rented reach is fine as long as you're converting some of it into owned reach.

## How does content longevity compare on Facebook vs YouTube?
This is YouTube's biggest structural advantage, and it's worth understanding precisely.

A Facebook post's useful life is measured in hours — sometimes a day or two for exceptional hits. The feed is forward-moving even when it's algorithmic: new pushes out old. That forces a treadmill. To stay visible, you must keep publishing, because each post starts decaying the moment it goes live. Evergreen content barely exists on Facebook; timeliness is the currency.

A YouTube video's useful life is measured in years. Search traffic doesn't care when you uploaded; it cares whether your video answers the query better than the alternatives. Suggested-video traffic compounds as your library grows, because every new video becomes another entry point that can surface older ones. Creators routinely find that videos from two or three years ago still drive a meaningful share of new subscribers — the [small YouTube channel growth playbook](/blog/small-youtube-channel-growth-playbook) leans on exactly this compounding effect.

The strategy implication is direct: on Facebook, optimize for volume and timeliness — consistent output that rides current conversations. On YouTube, optimize for durability — titles, topics, and thumbnails designed to be as clickable in three years as they are today. One platform rewards the news cycle; the other rewards the library. Know which game you're playing before you complain about the score.

## What content formats win on each platform?
Match the format to the platform's strengths and you'll stop fighting the algorithm.

YouTube is the home of depth: tutorials, reviews, essays, documentaries, vlogs — anything where watch time is the point. Long-form video lets you build authority and ad inventory simultaneously. Shorts work well as a discovery funnel, but they serve the long-form library, not the other way around.

Facebook rewards the quick and the communal: relatable clips, Reels, discussion posts that invite opinions, and live streams — especially lives inside Groups, where the audience is pre-assembled and notifications actually fire. Entertainment, humor, life hacks, and emotionally resonant stories travel furthest here.

Live video deserves a special note because it works on both, differently. Facebook Live thrives on community energy — Q&As in your group, behind-the-scenes hangs, real-time reactions to events. YouTube Live suits scheduled events — premieres, launches, shows with replay value — since the replay becomes a searchable asset afterward.

If your content is educational or evergreen, YouTube is probably your primary home. If it's entertaining, relatable, or community-driven, Facebook probably is. Most creators aren't purely one or the other, which brings us to the real answer to Facebook vs YouTube.

## Can you build on Facebook and YouTube without burning out?
Yes — but not by doubling your workload. The creators who sustain both platforms don't make twice the content; they make content work twice.

The core move is repurposing with intent. Record one long-form video for YouTube, then cut the strongest 30 to 60 seconds into a Reel for Facebook (and a Short while you're at it). Turn the filming process into behind-the-scenes Stories. Turn the best comments into a follow-up post. One recording session becomes a week's footprint across both platforms. Our guide on [how to repurpose content across platforms](/blog/how-to-repurpose-content-across-platforms) maps this workflow step by step.

Two rules keep it sustainable. First, pick a primary platform — the one where your best content naturally lives — and treat the other as distribution, not a second full-time job. Second, batch your work: film multiple videos in one day, edit in another block, and schedule everything with a [content calendar for creators](/blog/content-calendar-for-creators) so you're never creating under daily deadline panic.

The Facebook vs YouTube question was never really either-or. It's "which one first, and how does the second serve the first?" Answer that, and the two platforms stop competing for your time and start compounding each other's results.
