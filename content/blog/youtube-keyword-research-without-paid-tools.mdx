## Quick answer

You can do YouTube keyword research for free using autocomplete, competitor tags, and your own analytics — paid tools are convenient but not required. Start with autocomplete to find real searches, then validate demand against the competition. Once you have your keywords, our [YouTube tags guide](/blog/youtube-tags-guide) shows how to turn them into a proper tag set for each video.

Paid keyword tools will happily charge you $50 to $100 a month to tell you what people search for on YouTube. Here's what they don't advertise: YouTube hands you most of that data for free. The search bar, your competitors' tags, your own analytics — it's all sitting there, no subscription required.

I've used paid tools. They're convenient, but nothing below will surprise anyone who does the free methods properly. Here are seven of them, each with exact steps.

## How do you do YouTube keyword research for free?

Start with YouTube autocomplete: type a topic into the search bar without hitting enter — those suggestions are real searches from real viewers, ranked by popularity. Work systematically by adding letters ("sourdough bread a," "sourdough bread b") and question words ("sourdough bread how"), then use the wildcard trick ("how to _ sourdough") to reveal phrases you'd never think of. Write down everything without judging — you're building raw material.

Do it systematically. Take a seed keyword like "sourdough bread" and type it, then try it with each letter of the alphabet: "sourdough bread a," "sourdough bread b," and so on. Then try it with question words: "sourdough bread how," "sourdough bread why," "sourdough bread can." Each variation surfaces a new batch of real searches.

Then use the wildcard trick. Type your seed with an underscore or asterisk, which YouTube treats as a blank: "how to _ sourdough" or "best * for sourdough beginners." This reveals the words people put in the middle of phrases — the variations you'd never think of on your own.

Write down everything. Don't judge yet. You're building raw material, and volume matters at this stage.

## Method 2: Extract your competitors' tags

Here's something most creators don't realize: every YouTube video has tags, and while YouTube hides them from viewers, they're sitting in the page source. Studying the tags on successful videos in your niche shows you exactly which keywords the winners are targeting. Pair tag extraction with a full [competitor analysis](/blog/youtube-competitor-analysis-guide) for the complete picture.

The manual way is tedious — view page source, search for "keywords," copy the list. The fast way is our [YouTube Tag Extractor](/tools/youtube-tag-extractor): paste any video URL and it pulls the tag list instantly.

Do this for five to ten top-performing videos on your topic. You're not looking to copy anyone's tags verbatim. You're looking for *patterns*: the phrases that appear across multiple successful videos. If seven out of ten sourdough videos all tag "sourdough starter from scratch," that's a keyword with proven demand. Add it to your list.

## Method 3: Mine the comments for video ideas

Scroll to the comments on popular videos in your niche — not to read praise, but to hunt for questions. "Can you do a video on maintaining a starter in a cold climate?" "What flour ratio do you use?" Every one of those questions is a keyword handed to you directly by your target audience, complete with proof that someone wants the answer.

Sort comments by "Top comments" first, since the most-liked questions represent the most common curiosities. Then check the video's community posts and your own comments if you have any. Keep a running document of every question you find. After a month of this habit, you'll have more video ideas than you can film — each one pre-validated by real demand.

## Method 4: Google Trends with the YouTube filter

Go to Google Trends, enter your topic, and change the search type from "Web Search" to "YouTube Search." Now you're looking at actual YouTube search interest over time, and it tells you three useful things.

First, **trajectory**: is interest in this topic rising, flat, or dying? Making a video about a declining topic is an uphill battle. Second, **seasonality**: "sourdough" spikes every winter holiday season; "cold brew" spikes every summer. Time your videos to ride the wave. Third, **comparisons**: enter up to five terms and see which gets more search interest. "Sourdough starter" vs "sourdough recipe" vs "sourdough discard recipes" — the comparison settles which angle deserves your next video.

Also check the "Related queries" section at the bottom and flip it to "Rising." Those breakout terms are topics gaining traction right now, often before the competition notices.

## Method 5: Search filters to find underserved topics

This one finds gaps — topics with real viewer interest but weak existing videos. Search a broad term in your niche on YouTube, then open the Filters menu and set "Upload date" to "This month" and sort by "View count."

What you're looking for: videos uploaded recently that already have disproportionate views. A video from three weeks ago with 80,000 views on a topic where everything else is years old is a signal flare. It means viewers are hungry for this topic *right now* and the existing supply is stale. That's your opening — make the definitive, up-to-date version.

Repeat this monthly. It takes ten minutes and it's one of the highest-value habits in this entire list.

## Method 6: Your own YouTube Studio search data

If you've published even a handful of videos, YouTube is already telling you what people search to find you. In YouTube Studio, go to Analytics, then the Reach tab, and look at "Traffic source: YouTube search." Expand it and you'll see the exact search terms that led viewers to your videos.

This data is gold for two reasons. First, it reveals keywords you rank for *accidentally* — terms you never targeted but YouTube associated with your content. Those are proven winners for your specific channel; make dedicated videos for them. Second, it shows you the language your audience actually uses, which often differs from the jargon you'd naturally choose. If viewers search "bread keeps coming out flat" instead of "sourdough oven spring troubleshooting," use their words in your titles.

## Method 7: Reddit, Quora, and niche forums

Question-based keywords live on forums. Search Reddit for your niche's subreddits and sort by "Top" posts of the past year. The most-upvoted posts are the community's greatest hits — the questions and problems that resonate most. Each one is a video topic with a built-in audience.

On Quora, search your topic and look at which questions have the most followers and answers. High follower counts mean many people want the answer but few good ones exist. Niche forums (baking forums, photography forums, coding discords) work the same way — lurk, collect the recurring questions, and turn the most common ones into videos.

The bonus: when you publish, you can share the video back in those communities as a genuine answer, which drives your first wave of views from exactly the right audience.

## Worked example: building a keyword list from scratch

Let's run all seven methods on a hypothetical niche: budget home coffee setups. Seed keyword: "budget coffee setup."

**Autocomplete** gives us: "budget coffee setup 2026," "budget coffee setup under $200," "budget espresso setup for beginners," "budget coffee setup reddit."

**Competitor tags** (extracted from five popular videos) repeat: "cheap espresso setup," "budget home cafe," "coffee station ideas," "best budget grinder."

**Comment mining** surfaces: "what grinder under $100 is actually good?" "is a $150 espresso machine worth it?" "can you do an update with current prices?"

**Google Trends** shows "cold brew" rising into summer and "espresso" holding steady year-round.

**Search filters** reveal a three-week-old video, "I Built a Coffee Bar for $180," already at 95,000 views — a clear gap signal.

**Studio data** (pretending we have a small channel) shows we're accidentally ranking for "cheap coffee grinder" — a dedicated video is warranted.

**Reddit** (r/coffee, top posts) confirms the eternal debate: "best setup under $200."

Consolidated and prioritized, our keyword list becomes: "budget espresso setup under $200" (primary — specific, commercial, gap-confirmed), "best budget coffee grinder," "budget home coffee bar setup," "cheap espresso machine worth it," "budget cold brew setup." Five validated keywords, zero dollars spent.
