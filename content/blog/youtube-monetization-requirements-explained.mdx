## Quick answer

YouTube monetization requires 1,000 subscribers plus either 4,000 valid public watch hours in 12 months or 10 million valid public Shorts views in 90 days. Note: YouTube has announced that from February 1, 2027, new applicants will need 8,000 watch hours or 20 million Shorts views instead. Verify the current requirements on YouTube's official Partner Program pages.

This is one of the most common questions creators ask: "I have 900 subscribers — can I monetize yet?" The actual rules are simple, but they're buried under layers of myths about Shorts views, watch hours, and shortcuts that don't exist.

Here's the full picture of YouTube monetization requirements, explained plainly, as they stand in 2026. One important note up front: YouTube can and does adjust these thresholds, so treat this as your complete current guide — and double-check YouTube's official Partner Program pages before making big plans around specific numbers.

## The Main Path: Full YouTube Partner Program

To earn ad revenue from your videos, you need to join the YouTube Partner Program (YPP). The requirements:

- **1,000 subscribers**, AND
- **Either** 4,000 valid public watch hours on long-form videos in the last 12 months, **or** 10 million valid public Shorts views in the last 90 days. (YouTube has announced these double to 8,000 hours / 20 million Shorts views for new applicants from February 1, 2027.)

Plus the compliance basics: follow all YouTube monetization policies, have no active Community Guidelines strikes, enable 2-step verification on your Google account, and link an approved AdSense account.

That's it. There's no hidden subscriber-to-view ratio, no minimum video count, no requirement to be in a specific country beyond the ones where YPP operates (which covers most of the world, but verify yours).

Most creators take the watch-hours path. The Shorts path exists because Shorts generate views enormously faster than long-form — but as we'll see, those views pay very differently.

## The Early-Access Path: Fan Funding

YouTube also offers a lower tier that unlocks fan-funding features — channel memberships, Super Chat, Super Stickers, and [Super Thanks](/blog/youtube-super-thanks-guide) — without full ad revenue. The thresholds:

- **500 subscribers**, AND
- **3 valid public uploads in the last 90 days**, AND
- **Either** 3,000 valid public watch hours in the last 12 months **or** 3 million valid public Shorts views in the last 90 days.

This is genuinely useful. A small, loyal audience can generate meaningful membership and Super Chat income long before you hit 1,000 subscribers. If your content inspires strong community — live streams, niche expertise, entertainment — don't ignore this tier while you work toward full YPP.

## What counts toward YouTube monetization requirements?

Only watch hours from *public* videos count — unlisted and private videos contribute nothing, and deleting a video wipes its accumulated hours. The 12-month window is rolling ("the last 365 days"), so hours from 13 months ago have expired. Critically, Shorts views don't count toward the 4,000 watch hours — the two paths are separate. Subscribers must be genuine (sub4sub and bought subscribers get rejected), and views from ads or bots don't count.

This is where most confusion lives. Let's be precise:

**Watch hours:** Only hours from *public* videos count. Unlisted and private videos contribute nothing. If you delete a video, its accumulated hours disappear from your total. Live streams do count while public. The 12-month window is *rolling* — it's always "the last 365 days," so hours you earned 13 months ago have expired.

**Shorts views don't count toward the 4,000 watch hours.** This trips up so many creators. You can have 50 million Shorts views and zero progress toward the watch-hour threshold. The two paths are separate — pick the one that matches your content.

**Subscribers must be genuine.** Sub4sub schemes, bought subscribers, and engagement pods will get your application rejected — and if discovered later, can get your channel terminated. YouTube's systems are very good at detecting inauthentic growth. There is no shortcut here that survives review.

**Watch time must come from real viewers.** Views from ads don't count toward thresholds. Embedded views on external sites generally count if they're legitimate, but artificially inflated watch time from bots or click farms is detected and disqualified.

## Realistic Timelines: The Math

"How long will it take me?" Let's work it out honestly instead of guessing.

**Scenario: the consistent tutorial channel.** You publish 2 videos per week. Each averages 2,000 views with an average view duration of 5 minutes.

- Per video: 2,000 × 5 minutes = 10,000 minutes ≈ 167 watch hours
- Per week: ~333 hours
- Time to 4,000 hours: about 12 weeks of *sustained* performance

But that's the optimistic version where every video performs at your average from day one. In reality, your first 20-30 videos will likely underperform while the algorithm figures out who to show your content to. A realistic timeline for a consistent, decent-quality channel is **6 to 18 months** to reach full YPP. Channels in searchable niches (tutorials, how-tos) tend to get there faster because search delivers steady compounding views. Personality-driven channels often take longer because they depend on browse traffic and audience building.

**Scenario: the Shorts path.** 10 million Shorts views in 90 days sounds enormous — and it is — but a single viral Short can do 2-5 million views. Creators posting 1-2 Shorts daily in viral-friendly niches sometimes hit this in 2-4 months. The tradeoff, covered below, is what those views pay.

The uncomfortable truth: most channels that quit do so in months 3-6, right before compounding kicks in. The timeline isn't a talent problem for most people — it's a patience problem.

## The Review Process and Common Rejections

Hitting the numbers doesn't auto-approve you. YouTube reviews your channel, and rejections are common. The top reasons:

**Reused content.** This is the #1 rejection cause. It means your channel mostly republishes other people's content without adding significant original commentary or educational value — reaction videos with minimal commentary, compilations, reuploaded clips, "top 10" videos built entirely on others' footage. YouTube's policy is clear: the *majority* of your content should be original, and compilations need transformative commentary throughout, not just an intro and outro.

**Repetitive content.** Templated, mass-produced videos that are barely distinguishable from each other — automated slideshows, slightly varied versions of the same video published repeatedly. This policy targets low-effort content farms, and it's enforced more strictly every year.

**Invalid traffic.** Bought views, botted watch time, sub4sub. If your analytics show patterns of inauthentic activity, you'll be rejected — and repeated attempts can lead to channel-level penalties.

If you're rejected, YouTube tells you the general reason. Fix the actual underlying issue (not just the numbers), wait out the reapplication period, and apply again. Creators get approved on second and third attempts regularly — the review is about your channel's *current* state, not a permanent black mark.

Getting monetized is the starting line, not the finish. What you earn depends on your RPM — revenue per 1,000 views — which varies enormously by niche. Finance channels might see $15-25 RPMs; gaming channels might see $2-5. Geography matters too: US and UK viewers generate far more ad revenue than viewers in lower-ad-spend regions.
