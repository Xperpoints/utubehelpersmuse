## Quick answer

The right YouTube video length is however long your audience keeps watching — check your average view duration and retention graphs, not a universal number. Make the video as long as the topic needs, then cut everything that does not earn its place.

If you search for the ideal YouTube video length, you'll get a number. Eight minutes. Ten minutes. Fifteen. These numbers are delivered with total confidence and almost no evidence behind them.

Here is the honest answer, backed by what retention data consistently shows: the right length is however long your audience actually keeps watching. That sounds like a dodge, but it's the most useful thing anyone will tell you on this topic — because once you understand the retention math, you can find your own number instead of borrowing someone else's.

## Should your videos be over 8 minutes long?

Only if the content deserves it. The 8-minute rule is a monetization threshold — YouTube allows mid-roll ads on videos at least 8 minutes long — not a finding about human attention spans. Stretching thin content to 8 minutes tanks retention, which costs you more in reach than the mid-rolls earn.

Let's kill the most persistent myth first. The famous 8-minute rule has nothing to do with viewer psychology. YouTube allows mid-roll ads on videos that are at least 8 minutes long. That's it. It's a monetization threshold, not a finding about human attention spans.

Somewhere along the way, "videos over 8 minutes can show mid-roll ads" mutated into "videos should be 8 minutes long." Those are completely different statements. Stretching 4 minutes of real content into 8 minutes of filler doesn't help you — it tanks your retention, which hurts your reach, which costs you far more than the mid-roll revenue you were chasing.

Make the video as long as the content deserves. If it naturally crosses 8 minutes, take the mid-rolls as a bonus.

## The Metric That Actually Matters: Retention

YouTube doesn't promote long videos. It promotes videos that keep people on YouTube. The metric that captures this is **average percentage viewed** — what share of your video the typical viewer watches — combined with total watch time.

Here's why this reframes the length question entirely. Compare:

- **Video A:** 12 minutes long, 45% average viewed → 5.4 minutes of watch time per view
- **Video B:** 7 minutes long, 65% average viewed → 4.6 minutes of watch time per view

Video A generates more watch time per viewer despite worse retention percentage. But Video B's audience is more satisfied per minute, more likely to watch the next video, and more likely to subscribe. Both metrics matter, and YouTube weighs them together along with session time — whether your video leads to *more* YouTube watching afterward.

The practical takeaway: a useful rule of thumb is to aim for **50% or higher average percentage viewed**. If you're consistently above that, your length is working. If you're consistently below 35-40%, you have a length problem — or more precisely, a content-density problem. We'll get to testing your own number shortly.

## Starting Benchmarks by Content Type

These aren't rules. They're starting points based on what tends to work for each format — the length range where retention typically holds up:

- **Tutorials and how-tos:** 8-14 minutes. Viewers arrive with a specific problem and will stay as long as you're solving it efficiently. Rambling kills these fast.
- **Product reviews:** 6-12 minutes. Cover the key points, show the product in action, give a verdict. Most viewers decide in the first 3 minutes whether your review is worth finishing.
- **Vlogs:** 8-15 minutes. Personality carries these, but even loyal audiences have limits. Edit ruthlessly.
- **Video essays and documentaries:** 15-30 minutes. This audience *wants* depth — but only if the pacing earns it. Every minute needs to justify itself.
- **News and commentary:** 8-12 minutes. Timeliness matters more than length here.
- **Gaming and entertainment:** highly variable, 10-20 minutes is common, but retention curves in entertainment are brutal — the first 60 seconds decide everything.

Notice the pattern: the right length is set by the *job the video does*, not by the platform. A tutorial that answers the question in 6 minutes shouldn't be 12. An essay that needs 22 minutes shouldn't be cut to 10.

## The Retention Math, Worked Out

Let me show you how to actually think about this with a real scenario.

Say you make cooking tutorials. Your last five videos averaged 11 minutes with 42% average viewed — that's about 4.6 minutes of watch time per view. You wonder if shorter would do better.

You publish a tighter 7-minute version of a similar recipe. It gets 58% average viewed — about 4.1 minutes per view. Slightly less watch time per view, but here's what else changed: click-through rate stayed the same, while the percentage of viewers who watched your end screen and clicked to the next video jumped from 3% to 6%.

That second number matters enormously. YouTube's strongest signal isn't just how long someone watched *your* video — it's whether your video extended their total session. A shorter video that reliably funnels viewers into a second video can outperform a longer one on the metric that actually drives promotion.

This is why copying someone else's "ideal length" is useless. Your audience, your format, your pacing — the math is yours to do.

## How to Find YOUR Ideal Length: A Testing Method

Here's a simple protocol. It takes about a month, and it gives you an answer no guru can:

**Step 1: Establish your baseline.** Look at your last 8-10 videos in YouTube Studio. Note the average percentage viewed for each, along with the video length. You're looking for your current normal.

**Step 2: Change one variable.** Pick a proven topic — something you've covered before or that's performed well. Make the next video deliberately tighter (or fuller) than usual. Change *only* the length/density, not the topic, thumbnail style, or title format. If you change everything at once, you'll learn nothing.

**Step 3: Compare relative retention, not raw views.** In Studio, open the video's analytics and check *relative* audience retention — how your video holds viewers compared to similar-length videos. This controls for the fact that longer videos naturally have lower percentage retention. If your tighter video beats your channel average on relative retention, shorter is your direction.

**Step 4: Repeat 3-4 times.** One video is noise — it might have just been a better topic. Three or four in the new length gives you a signal. The length range where your relative retention is consistently strongest is your answer.

Two caveats: give each test video at least a week before judging (early data skews toward subscribers, who watch differently than browse viewers), and don't test during holidays or major events when viewing behavior is weird.

## When Longer Wins, When Shorter Wins

Longer wins when the topic has genuine depth and the audience arrives invested: in-depth tutorials, essays, documentaries, detailed comparisons. These viewers *want* you to be thorough — cutting corners frustrates them.

Shorter wins when the answer is simple, the audience is new, or the format is entertainment. A "how to fix this error" video should be exactly as long as the fix takes. A comedy sketch should end before the joke wears thin. And if most of your traffic comes from browse and suggested — viewers who didn't choose your topic deliberately — tighter almost always outperforms.

There's also a packaging interaction most creators miss: **your title and thumbnail set a length expectation.** "The Complete Guide to..." promises depth and buys you patience. "Fix This in 60 Seconds" promises speed — go over 3 minutes and viewers feel cheated, no matter how good the content is. Match your length to your promise.

## The Bottom Line

Stop asking "how long should my videos be" and start asking "how long do my viewers stay, and why do they leave." The answer to the second question — found in your retention graphs and a month of deliberate testing — gives you the first.
