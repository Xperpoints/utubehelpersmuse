## Quick answer

To start a YouTube channel in 2026, pick a sustainable niche, set up clear branding, and plan your first 10 videos before uploading. You can start with a phone and free editing software — consistency and packaging matter far more than gear.

Every week, thousands of people start YouTube channels. Most of them quit within three months — not because they lacked talent, but because they started without a plan. They uploaded five random videos, got eleven views each, and concluded "YouTube is saturated."

YouTube isn't saturated. Bad planning is just common. This guide is the plan every new creator wishes someone had handed them: choosing a niche that can actually grow, branding that looks intentional from day one, gear that costs nothing, and a concrete roadmap for the first 10 videos. Follow it, and you'll skip the mistakes that kill most new channels.

## Step 1: Pick a niche you can sustain for 100 videos

This is the decision that matters most, and most beginners get it backwards. They ask "what's profitable?" or "what's trending?" instead of the only question that matters: **"What can I make 100 videos about without getting bored?"**

YouTube rewards consistency over months and years. A niche you're excited about in week one but sick of by week eight is a dead channel. Before committing, run every niche idea through three filters:

**Filter 1: Do you have genuine interest or experience?** You don't need to be an expert — being a beginner documenting your learning is a valid angle — but you need enough curiosity to research topics weekly for a year.

**Filter 2: Is there a specific audience?** "Gaming" is not a niche; it's a continent. "Budget PC building for students" is a niche. You should be able to describe your viewer in one sentence: "A 22-year-old who wants to build a gaming PC for under $600."

**Filter 3: Can you name 30 video ideas right now?** If you can't brainstorm 30 topics off the top of your head, you'll run dry by month three. This is the filter most people skip, and it's the one that predicts survival.

### Niches that work well for beginners in 2026

You don't need a "secret" niche — you need a clear one. Some consistently strong starting points:

- **Skill tutorials in your profession** (Excel for accountants, Notion for students, editing for beginners)
- **Budget versions of expensive hobbies** (budget filmmaking, cheap home workouts, thrift flipping)
- **Local content** (your city's food, travel, real estate — low competition by definition)
- **Documenting a learning journey** (learning to code, getting fit, learning a language)
- **Tool and software tutorials** (new apps and AI tools always need explainers)

Avoid picking a niche purely because a guru said it's "high CPM." [Finance pays well per view](/blog/profitable-youtube-niches-2026), but it's brutally competitive — a smaller niche you can dominate beats a lucrative niche where you're invisible.

## Step 2: Brand it like you mean it

Your channel needs to look intentional from video one. Not expensive — intentional. Here's the minimum viable branding:

**Channel name:** Keep it short, spellable, and searchable. Avoid numbers, underscores, and clever misspellings — if someone hears your name on a podcast, they should be able to type it into YouTube and find you. If you're stuck, our [YouTube Channel Name Generator](/tools/youtube-channel-name-generator) can spark ideas.

**Profile picture and banner:** A clear face photo (for [personal brands](/blog/personal-branding-for-creators)) or a simple logo (for topical channels). Your banner should state what the channel is about in one line — "Budget PC builds every Tuesday" beats a generic mountain landscape. Use our [Channel Banner Generator](/tools/youtube-channel-banner-generator) to get the dimensions and layout right.

**Channel description:** Two to three sentences: who the channel is for, what you publish, and when. Include an upload schedule — even "new videos every Wednesday" builds trust.

**Trailer:** A 60-90 second channel trailer pinned to your homepage. State who you are, who the channel is for, and what they'll get. Most beginners skip this; it's the first thing a curious visitor watches.

Total cost of all of this: zero. Total time: one afternoon. But it separates you from the 90% of new channels that look like accidents.

## Do you need expensive gear to start a YouTube channel?

No. Any smartphone from the last 3–4 years shoots 1080p video that's more than good enough, a window gives you lighting that beats most ring lights, and free editors like DaVinci Resolve or CapCut are enough to look professional. The one area worth a small spend later is audio — viewers forgive bad video but not bad audio. Rule of thumb: don't spend a dollar until you've published 20 videos.

The biggest lie in beginner YouTube advice is that you need equipment. Here's what you actually need:

**Camera:** Your phone. Any smartphone from the last 3-4 years shoots 1080p video that's more than good enough. I know channels with 100K+ subscribers still shooting on phones. Upgrade only when your content — not your insecurity — demands it.

**Audio:** This is the one area worth a tiny investment, because viewers forgive bad video but not bad audio. Start free: record in a small, quiet room with soft furnishings (closets work shockingly well), and get the phone's mic close to your mouth. When you're ready to spend, a $20-30 lavalier mic is the best value upgrade in all of YouTube.

**Lighting:** A window. Seriously — face a window during the day and you have lighting that beats most ring lights. Film with the window in front of you, not behind you.

**Editing:** DaVinci Resolve (free, professional-grade) or CapCut (free, simpler). Both are enough to make videos that look completely professional. Editing skill matters 100x more than editing software.

**The rule:** Don't spend a dollar until you've published 20 videos. By then you'll know what you actually need — and you'll have proven to yourself that you'll stick around long enough for the investment to matter.

## Step 4: Your first 10 videos (the actual plan)

Here's where most beginners fail: they upload whatever comes to mind. Instead, plan your first 10 videos as a portfolio that does three jobs — teaches the algorithm who your audience is, gives new viewers a reason to subscribe, and teaches *you* how to make videos.

### Videos 1-3: Answer the most-searched questions in your niche

Go to YouTube search, type your niche, and note the autocomplete suggestions. Those are real searches from real people. Make videos answering the three most common beginner questions. These are your slow-burn search traffic videos — they won't explode, but they'll bring steady views for years.

Example for a budget filmmaking channel:
1. "What Camera Should a Beginner Buy in 2026? (Under $500)"
2. "5 Beginner Filmmaking Mistakes to Avoid"
3. "How to Get Cinematic Footage With a Phone"

### Videos 4-6: Show your process

Behind-the-scenes and process videos build connection. Film yourself doing the thing — editing a video, building the PC, cooking the dish — and talk through your decisions. These won't get the most views, but they'll get the most *subscribers per view*, because people subscribe to people, not tutorials.

### Videos 7-8: Your opinionated takes

Pick two debated topics in your niche and take a clear stance. "Why I Stopped Using [Popular Tool]" or "The [Niche] Advice I Disagree With." Opinion videos get comments, comments drive engagement, and engagement tells the algorithm people care.

### Videos 9-10: Your best ideas so far

By now you've published 8 videos and learned what resonates. Check your analytics — which topics got the best click-through and retention? Make videos 9 and 10 as improved, bigger versions of your winners. This is how channels find their format: publish, measure, double down.

### The production standard for all 10

- **Hook in the first 15 seconds.** State what the video delivers and why it matters. No "hey guys, welcome back to my channel" — you're not welcoming anyone back; they've never been here.
- **One idea per video.** Beginners cram three videos' worth of content into one and wonder why retention drops at minute four.
- **Edit ruthlessly.** Cut every "um," every repeated point, every tangent. A tight 8-minute video beats a rambling 20-minute one every time.
- **End with a reason to stay.** Point to a related video, not a generic "like and subscribe."

## Step 5: Publish consistently for 90 days before judging anything

Here's the timeline nobody wants to hear: your first 10-20 videos will probably get modest views. That's normal. The algorithm is learning who likes your content, you're learning how to make it, and your library is too small for the "suggested" engine to work with.

Commit to this: **one to two videos per week, same days, for 90 days.** Don't check analytics obsessively — check weekly. Don't compare yourself to channels in year three when you're in month one. And don't change your niche at video six because growth is slow. Niche-hopping resets the algorithm's learning every time.

After 90 days and roughly 15-25 videos, you'll have real data: which topics earned clicks, which held attention, who's watching. That's when strategy begins. Before that, it's just reps.

## A worked example: putting it all together

Let's build a channel from scratch. Meet our hypothetical creator: a 26-year-old accountant who loves meal prepping on Sundays.

- **Niche (3 filters):** Genuine interest? Yes — she meal preps weekly. Specific audience? "Busy professionals who want healthy weekday lunches without cooking daily." 30 ideas? Easy: chicken recipes, vegetarian weeks, budget prep, container reviews, 15-minute prep methods...
- **Name:** "Prep Sunday" — short, spellable, says what it is.
- **Banner:** "Healthy weekday lunches, prepped every Sunday" + upload schedule.
- **Gear:** Phone on a $15 tripod, kitchen window light, free CapCut.
- **First 10 videos:**
  1. "5 Chicken Meal Prep Recipes for the Whole Week"
  2. "Meal Prep Containers: What's Actually Worth Buying"
  3. "How to Meal Prep in 90 Minutes (Full Sunday Routine)"
  4. "Come Grocery Shopping With Me: $60 Weekly Haul"
  5. "My Exact Sunday Prep Process, Start to Finish"
  6. "3 Meal Prep Mistakes I Made for a Year"
  7. "Why I Stopped Meal Prepping Breakfasts"
  8. "High-Protein vs Budget Prep: Which Is Better?"
  9. "5 NEW Chicken Recipes (You Asked For More)" — doubling down on video 1's winner
  10. "The Ultimate 90-Minute Prep: Level 2" — bigger version of video 3

Every video serves the same audience. The algorithm learns fast. By video 10, she knows exactly what her viewers want more of.

Starting a YouTube channel in 2026 comes down to five decisions: a niche you can sustain, branding that looks intentional, gear that costs nothing, a planned first 10 videos, and 90 days of consistency before you judge results. None of it requires money or luck — just a plan and the discipline to follow it.
